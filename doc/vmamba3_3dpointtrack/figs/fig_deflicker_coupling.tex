  {\textbf{(c) What trains it.} As built there is no de-flicker loss; the stage learns only through
   the tracking loss:};
\node[anchor=west] at (0.4,-0.55)
  {$L=\underbrace{vis\cdot|xyz_{\text{pred}}-xyz_{\text{gt}}|_1/\text{scale}_{\text{gt}}}
      _{\text{per-point; the stage cannot change this}}
    +\ \lambda_{uv}\,vis\,|\Delta uv|^2$};
\node[anchor=west, text width=15.5cm] at (0,-1.6)
  {The stage emits one scalar per frame, but the loss sums over ${\sim}900$ points $\times$ $F$
   frames, and a single per-frame scalar accounts for only $0.3$--$0.6\%$ of that error
   (\S\ref{sec:deflicker-signal}). Roughly $99.5\%$ of its gradient is therefore noise from its
   point of view. A direct target, $\Delta s^{*}_f=\mathrm{median}_n[\log z_{\text{gt}}-\log
   z_{\text{raw}}]$ with $L_{\text{dsr}}=|\Delta s_f-\Delta s^{*}_f|$, supervises exactly what it
   can do.};
\end{scope}

\caption{Why the de-flicker stage depends on the optical-flow front-end, and why its training signal
  is weak. Orange is data, blue is trainable, grey is a frozen component or an intermediate. The
  stage's job is to undo DA3-g's per-frame global scale drift, which is a property of the depth
  model alone --- yet as built (a) it observes that drift only through points the tracker selected,
  which is what couples it to the front-end and makes a SEA-RAFT-trained stage behave differently
  from a WAFT-trained one on identical depth.}
